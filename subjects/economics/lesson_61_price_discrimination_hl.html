Lesson 61: Price Discrimination

What is Price Discrimination?

Price discrimination occurs when a firm charges different prices to different customers for the same product, where the price difference is not based on cost differences.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand the key concepts in Price Discrimination
  • Explain what is price discrimination?
  • Explain degrees of price discrimination
  • Explain the economics of third-degree price discrimination
  • Apply your knowledge through practice problems and examples

Conditions for Price Discrimination

Three conditions must be met:

  1. Market power: The firm must have some control over price (monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition)
  2. Market segmentation: The firm must be able to identify and separate customer groups with different price elasticities of demand
  3. No resale: Customers buying at low prices cannot resell to those charged high prices (arbitrage must be prevented)

Degrees of Price Discrimination

First-Degree (Perfect) Price Discrimination

The firm charges each customer the maximum they are willing to pay. The firm captures all consumer surplus as producer surplus.

Examples: Auctions, personalized pricing algorithms, negotiated prices (e.g., car sales)

Efficiency: Allocatively efficient (no deadweight loss) but highly inequitable

Second-Degree Price Discrimination

The firm charges different prices based on quantity consumed or product version. Customers self-select into groups.

Examples:

  • Quantity discounts (bulk buying)
  • Two-part tariffs (membership fee + per-unit price)
  • Versioning (economy, business, first-class airline seats)

Third-Degree Price Discrimination

The firm segments the market and charges different prices to different groups based on identifiable characteristics.

Examples:

  • Age-based pricing (student, senior discounts)
  • Geographic pricing (different prices in different countries)
  • Time-based pricing (peak vs. off-peak electricity, matinee movie prices)

The Economics of Third-Degree Price Discrimination

The firm maximizes profit by setting marginal revenue equal to marginal cost in each market segment:

$$MR_1 = MR_2 = MC$$

Where $MR_1$ and $MR_2$ are marginal revenues in markets 1 and 2.

Since $MR = P(1 - \frac{1}{|PED|})$, this implies:

$$P_1(1 - \frac{1}{|PED_1|}) = P_2(1 - \frac{1}{|PED_2|})$$

Key insight: The market with less elastic demand is charged a higher price.

Example: Airline Pricing

Business travelers have inelastic demand (must travel, company pays) → charged high prices.

Leisure travelers have elastic demand (price-sensitive, flexible dates) → charged lower prices.

Airlines use Saturday-night stay requirements and advance purchase rules to separate these groups.

Welfare Effects

Producer Surplus

Price discrimination always increases producer surplus (profit) compared to single pricing — that's why firms do it.

Consumer Surplus

Effect on consumer surplus is ambiguous:

  • Some consumers pay more (those with inelastic demand) → lose surplus
  • Some consumers pay less (those with elastic demand who wouldn't buy at single price) → gain surplus

Total Welfare

Price discrimination can reduce deadweight loss by allowing more transactions to occur. Some customers who would be priced out under single pricing can now afford the product at a lower price.

Perfect price discrimination is allocatively efficient but transfers all surplus to producers.

Examples in Practice

Pharmaceuticals: Drug companies charge higher prices in wealthy countries (inelastic demand) and lower prices in developing countries (elastic demand).
Streaming Services: Different subscription prices in different countries based on local willingness to pay.
Universities: Financial aid allows price discrimination based on ability to pay, increasing enrollment while maximizing revenue.

Policy Considerations

Arguments Against Price Discrimination

  • Inequitable: Transfers surplus from consumers to producers
  • May exploit vulnerable groups
  • Can create market barriers

Arguments For Price Discrimination

  • Increases access for price-sensitive consumers
  • Reduces deadweight loss
  • May fund cross-subsidization (e.g., unprofitable rural services)
  • Enables provision of public goods (e.g., museums offering free admission to some groups)

Summary

Price discrimination involves charging different prices for reasons unrelated to costs:

  • Requires market power, market segmentation, and no resale
  • Three degrees: perfect (each customer), second-degree (quantity/version), third-degree (groups)
  • Firms charge higher prices to groups with less elastic demand
  • Increases producer surplus, ambiguous effect on consumer surplus and total welfare
  • Can improve efficiency by reducing deadweight loss but raises equity concerns
