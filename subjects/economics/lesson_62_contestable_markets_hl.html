Lesson 62: Contestable Markets Theory

Introduction to Contestability

Contestable markets theory, developed by William Baumol (1982), challenges traditional market structure analysis by arguing that the threat of entry can discipline incumbent firms even in concentrated markets.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand the key concepts in Contestable Markets Theory
  • Explain sunk costs: the key barrier
  • Explain the contestability result
  • Apply your knowledge through practice problems and examples

A perfectly contestable market is one where:

  • Entry is completely free (no barriers to entry)
  • Exit is costless (no sunk costs)
  • Potential entrants have access to the same technology as incumbents
  • Entry can occur before incumbents can react (hit-and-run entry)

Sunk Costs: The Key Barrier

Sunk costs are costs that cannot be recovered when exiting a market. They are the primary barrier to contestability.

Examples of Sunk Costs

  • High: Custom machinery, brand-building advertising, R&D specific to the product, specialized infrastructure
  • Low: Rented equipment, generic advertising, readily resalable assets

Markets with low sunk costs are highly contestable even if currently concentrated.

Example: Airline Routes

Before deregulation, airlines were thought to be natural monopolies. But aircraft can be moved between routes easily (low sunk costs), making routes contestable. A monopolist on a route must price competitively or face hit-and-run entry.

The Contestability Result

In a perfectly contestable market, even a monopolist must:

  • Set price at average cost ($P = AC$)
  • Earn zero economic profit
  • Operate at minimum efficient scale

Why? If the incumbent charges $P > AC$ and earns supernormal profit, a potential entrant can:

  1. Enter the market
  2. Undercut the incumbent slightly
  3. Capture the entire market
  4. Exit before the incumbent can respond

This "hit-and-run" strategy is profitable whenever $P > AC$. The incumbent, anticipating this, sets $P = AC$ preemptively.

Implications for Policy

Traditional View vs. Contestability

Traditional view: Concentration (few firms) implies market power and inefficiency → regulate or break up

Contestability view: Concentration is not a problem if the market is contestable → focus on removing barriers to entry, not firm numbers

Policy Focus

  • Reduce sunk costs through regulation (e.g., allow equipment leasing, standardize technology)
  • Lower legal/regulatory barriers (licensing, permits)
  • Ensure access to essential facilities (e.g., airport slots, telecommunications infrastructure)
  • Prevent incumbent predatory pricing

Criticisms and Limitations

Theoretical Criticisms

  • Timing assumption: In reality, incumbents can respond quickly (cut prices, increase output), making hit-and-run difficult
  • Customer loyalty: Brand loyalty and switching costs create barriers even without sunk costs
  • Reputation effects: Incumbents may build a reputation for aggressive responses to deter entry
  • Strategic behavior: Incumbents can engage in limit pricing or predatory pricing

Empirical Evidence

Airline deregulation provides mixed evidence:

  • For contestability: Many routes saw price decreases and increased service despite concentration
  • Against contestability: Incumbent advantages (frequent flier programs, hub control, gate access) created barriers; actual entry often required, not just threat

Real-World Applications

Technology Markets: Software and app markets are highly contestable (low sunk costs, digital distribution). Yet network effects and lock-in can create barriers.
Ridesharing: Uber/Lyft markets have low sunk costs (drivers use their own cars), making them contestable. Entry has occurred in many cities.
Natural Monopolies: Traditional utilities (water, electricity transmission) have very high sunk costs and are not contestable, justifying regulation.

Contestability vs. Perfect Competition

Feature Perfect Competition Contestability
Number of firms Many Can be few or one
Key discipline Actual competition Threat of entry
Long-run profit Zero Zero
Price P = MC P = AC (may be > MC)
Efficiency Allocatively efficient Productively efficient, not allocatively

Summary

Contestable markets theory offers important insights:

  • The threat of entry can constrain incumbent behavior even without actual competitors
  • Sunk costs are the key barrier to contestability
  • Contestable markets yield zero economic profit and productive efficiency
  • Policy should focus on lowering entry barriers, not breaking up concentrated industries
  • However, perfect contestability is rare due to incumbent advantages and strategic behavior

Understanding contestability helps explain why some concentrated markets perform competitively while others do not.
