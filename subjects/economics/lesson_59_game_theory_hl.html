Lesson 59: Game Theory and Strategic Behavior

Introduction to Game Theory

Game theory is the study of strategic decision-making where the outcome for each participant depends not only on their own actions but also on the actions of others. In oligopolistic markets, firms must anticipate rivals' reactions when making pricing, output, or advertising decisions.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand the key concepts in Game Theory and Strategic Behavior
  • Explain basic components of a game
  • Explain the prisoner's dilemma
  • Apply your knowledge through practice problems and examples

Game theory provides a framework for analyzing these interdependent decisions and predicting market outcomes. This is a key HL extension that helps explain why oligopolies behave differently from perfectly competitive or monopolistic markets.

Basic Components of a Game

Every game has three essential elements:

  • Players: The decision-makers (e.g., firms in an oligopoly)
  • Strategies: The possible actions each player can take
  • Payoffs: The outcomes resulting from each combination of strategies

The Prisoner's Dilemma

The Prisoner's Dilemma is the most famous game theory model and illustrates why rational individuals might not cooperate even when it's in their collective interest.

The Story

Two suspects are arrested and interrogated separately. The prosecutor offers each the same deal:

  • If both remain silent, each serves 1 year in prison (light evidence)
  • If both confess, each serves 5 years
  • If one confesses and the other remains silent, the confessor goes free while the silent one serves 10 years

Payoff Matrix

Prisoner B
Stay Silent Confess
Prisoner A Stay Silent -1, -1 -10, 0
Confess 0, -10 -5, -5

Note: Numbers represent years in prison (negative payoffs). First number is Prisoner A's outcome, second is Prisoner B's.

Dominant Strategy and Nash Equilibrium

A dominant strategy is one that yields a better payoff regardless of what the other player does.

In the Prisoner's Dilemma:

  • If B stays silent, A gets -1 (silent) or 0 (confess) → confess is better
  • If B confesses, A gets -10 (silent) or -5 (confess) → confess is better
  • Therefore, confessing is A's dominant strategy
  • By symmetry, confessing is also B's dominant strategy

A Nash equilibrium occurs when each player chooses their best strategy given the other player's choice, and no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally.

The Nash equilibrium is (Confess, Confess) with payoffs (-5, -5), even though both would be better off at (-1, -1) if they cooperated. This illustrates the conflict between individual rationality and collective welfare.

The Oligopoly Pricing Game

The Prisoner's Dilemma applies directly to oligopolistic pricing decisions. Consider two firms deciding whether to keep prices high or cut prices:

Firm B
High Price Low Price
Firm A High Price $100m, $100m $20m, $150m
Low Price $150m, $20m $60m, $60m

Analysis:

  • If both charge high prices: Each earns $100m (collusive outcome)
  • If both charge low prices: Each earns $60m (Nash equilibrium)
  • If one undercuts: The price-cutter gains market share and earns $150m while the other earns only $20m

Each firm's dominant strategy is to charge a low price, leading to a Nash equilibrium at (Low, Low) even though (High, High) would be mutually beneficial. This explains why:

  • Cartels are unstable — firms have incentives to cheat
  • Price wars can occur in oligopolies
  • Firms may prefer explicit or tacit collusion to avoid the low-price trap

Repeated Games and Cooperation

The analysis changes when firms interact repeatedly over time. In a repeated game, firms may cooperate to achieve the mutually beneficial outcome by using strategies like:

  • Tit-for-tat: Start by cooperating, then mirror the opponent's previous move
  • Trigger strategies: Cooperate until the rival cheats, then punish them permanently

Repeated interaction creates an incentive to maintain a good reputation and avoid triggering retaliation. This explains why stable oligopolies can maintain high prices without formal collusion.

Advertising Game

Game theory also explains advertising behavior in oligopolies:

Firm B
Don't Advertise Advertise
Firm A Don't Advertise $50m, $50m $20m, $70m
Advertise $70m, $20m $40m, $40m

The dominant strategy for both firms is to advertise, leading to the Nash equilibrium at (Advertise, Advertise) with profits of $40m each. Both would be better off not advertising ($50m each), but each fears losing market share if the rival advertises.

This explains why oligopolies often engage in heavy advertising even though it reduces overall industry profits — a form of "arms race."

The Kinked Demand Curve Revisited

Game theory provides a theoretical foundation for the kinked demand curve model. Firms expect that:

  • If they raise prices, rivals won't follow (lose market share)
  • If they cut prices, rivals will match (price war with little gain)

This asymmetric expectation about rivals' reactions creates a kink at the current price and explains price rigidity in oligopolies.

HL Exam Applications

You may be asked to:

  • Identify dominant strategies in a payoff matrix
  • Find the Nash equilibrium
  • Explain why the Nash equilibrium may not be the best collective outcome
  • Apply game theory to explain oligopoly behavior (pricing, advertising, collusion)
  • Discuss how repeated games affect the likelihood of cooperation

Summary

Game theory is essential for understanding strategic behavior in oligopolies:

  • The Prisoner's Dilemma shows why rational firms may not cooperate even when it would be mutually beneficial
  • Dominant strategies and Nash equilibria predict market outcomes
  • Game theory explains price wars, advertising races, and the instability of cartels
  • Repeated games increase the potential for cooperation through reputation effects

This framework provides deeper insight into why oligopolistic markets behave so differently from the competitive ideal.
