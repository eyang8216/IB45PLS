Lesson 58: Theory of the Firm (HL Extension)

Introduction to Advanced Firm Theory

At the Higher Level, IB Economics requires a deeper understanding of how firms make production and pricing decisions. While Standard Level introduces basic cost curves and market structures, HL students must analyze firms' behavior using mathematical relationships, understand long-run production decisions, and evaluate alternative objectives beyond profit maximization.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand the key concepts in Theory of the Firm (HL Extension)
  • Explain the production function
  • Explain marginal product and diminishing returns
  • Apply your knowledge through practice problems and examples

This lesson extends the SL theory of the firm by introducing the production function, exploring the relationship between short-run and long-run costs, and examining how firms choose optimal input combinations.

The Production Function

A production function shows the maximum output that can be produced from different combinations of inputs. For simplicity, we typically consider two inputs: labor ($L$) and capital ($K$).

$$Q = f(L, K)$$

where $Q$ is output quantity, $L$ is labor input, and $K$ is capital input.

Short Run vs. Long Run

The distinction between short run and long run is crucial:

  • Short run: At least one factor of production is fixed (typically capital). Firms can only vary labor.
  • Long run: All factors of production are variable. Firms can adjust both labor and capital.

In the short run, the production function becomes $Q = f(L, \bar{K})$ where $\bar{K}$ indicates fixed capital.

Marginal Product and Diminishing Returns

The marginal product of labor (MPL) measures the additional output from hiring one more unit of labor:

$$MPL = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta L}$$

Similarly, the marginal product of capital (MPK) is:

$$MPK = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta K}$$

The law of diminishing marginal returns states that as more units of a variable input (e.g., labor) are added to a fixed input (e.g., capital), the marginal product of the variable input eventually declines.

Isoquants and Isocosts

In the long run, firms can choose different combinations of labor and capital to produce the same output level. An isoquant shows all combinations of labor and capital that produce the same quantity of output.

Properties of Isoquants

  • Isoquants slope downward (trade-off between inputs)
  • Higher isoquants represent higher output levels
  • Isoquants are convex to the origin (diminishing marginal rate of technical substitution)
  • Isoquants cannot intersect

The slope of an isoquant is called the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS), which shows the rate at which one input can be substituted for another while keeping output constant:

$$MRTS_{L,K} = -\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta L} = \frac{MPL}{MPK}$$

Isocost Lines

An isocost line shows all combinations of labor and capital that cost the same total amount. If the wage rate is $w$ and the rental rate of capital is $r$, and the firm's total cost is $C$, then:

$$C = wL + rK$$

Rearranging to express capital as a function of labor:

$$K = \frac{C}{r} - \frac{w}{r}L$$

The slope of the isocost line is $-\frac{w}{r}$, showing the relative price of labor to capital.

Cost Minimization and Producer Equilibrium

A profit-maximizing firm seeks to produce a given output level at the lowest possible cost. This occurs where the isocost line is tangent to the isoquant — where the slopes are equal:

$$MRTS_{L,K} = \frac{w}{r}$$

Or equivalently:

$$\frac{MPL}{MPK} = \frac{w}{r}$$

Rearranging:

$$\frac{MPL}{w} = \frac{MPK}{r}$$

This condition states that the firm should hire inputs until the marginal product per dollar spent is equal across all inputs. If $\frac{MPL}{w} > \frac{MPK}{r}$, the firm should hire more labor and less capital.

Returns to Scale

Returns to scale describe what happens to output when all inputs are increased by the same proportion in the long run:

  • Constant returns to scale: Doubling all inputs doubles output. $f(2L, 2K) = 2 \cdot f(L, K)$
  • Increasing returns to scale: Doubling all inputs more than doubles output. $f(2L, 2K) > 2 \cdot f(L, K)$
  • Decreasing returns to scale: Doubling all inputs less than doubles output. $f(2L, 2K) < 2 \cdot f(L, K)$

Returns to scale explain the shape of the long-run average cost (LRAC) curve:

  • Increasing returns to scale → Economies of scale → LRAC falls
  • Constant returns to scale → Constant costs → LRAC is flat
  • Decreasing returns to scale → Diseconomies of scale → LRAC rises

Connection to Cost Curves

The mathematical relationship between marginal product and marginal cost is:

$$MC = \frac{w}{MPL}$$

This shows that when MPL is rising, MC is falling, and when MPL is falling (diminishing returns), MC is rising. This explains why the MC curve is U-shaped.

Similarly, average variable cost (AVC) is related to average product of labor (APL):

$$AVC = \frac{w}{APL}$$

HL Exam Applications

At HL, you may be asked to:

  • Calculate marginal products from production data
  • Determine the optimal input combination given input prices
  • Explain why a firm would substitute capital for labor when wages rise
  • Analyze how technological change shifts isoquants
  • Evaluate whether a firm exhibits increasing, constant, or decreasing returns to scale

Summary

This HL extension deepens your understanding of firm behavior by introducing the production function and showing how firms choose optimal combinations of inputs. Key concepts include:

  • Marginal product and the law of diminishing returns
  • Isoquants (combinations of inputs producing the same output)
  • Isocosts (combinations of inputs costing the same amount)
  • Producer equilibrium where MRTS = w/r
  • Returns to scale and their relationship to economies of scale

These analytical tools allow you to understand not just what firms do, but why they make particular production decisions.
