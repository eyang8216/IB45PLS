Lesson 60: Asymmetric Information and Market Failure

What is Asymmetric Information?

Asymmetric information occurs when one party in a transaction has more or better information than the other. This violates a key assumption of perfect competition (perfect information) and can lead to market failure.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand the key concepts in Asymmetric Information and Market Failure
  • Explain what is asymmetric information?
  • Explain adverse selection: the market for lemons
  • Explain moral hazard
  • Apply your knowledge through practice problems and examples

Two main problems arise from asymmetric information:

  • Adverse selection: Occurs before a transaction, when one party has hidden information
  • Moral hazard: Occurs after a transaction, when one party engages in hidden actions

Adverse Selection: The Market for Lemons

George Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" (1970) demonstrates how asymmetric information can cause markets to fail entirely.

The Used Car Market

Consider a used car market where:

  • Sellers know whether their car is good quality or poor quality ("lemon")
  • Buyers cannot distinguish between good cars and lemons before purchase
  • Good cars are worth $10,000 to buyers and $8,000 to sellers
  • Lemons are worth $5,000 to buyers and $4,000 to sellers

If buyers could identify quality, both types would trade at mutually beneficial prices. However, with asymmetric information:

  1. Buyers know half the cars are lemons, so they offer a price reflecting average quality: $7,500
  2. Sellers of good cars refuse to sell at $7,500 (below their $8,000 valuation)
  3. Only lemon owners accept $7,500
  4. Buyers realize only lemons are offered and lower their offer to $5,000
  5. Good cars are driven out of the market entirely — only lemons trade

This is called adverse selection because the information asymmetry adversely affects the quality mix in the market. The market outcome is inefficient: trade in good cars that would be mutually beneficial does not occur.

Real-World Example: Health Insurance

People know their own health status better than insurance companies. Those expecting high medical costs are more likely to buy insurance, while healthy people may opt out. This raises average costs and premiums, driving more healthy people out — potentially leading to market collapse.

Moral Hazard

Moral hazard occurs when one party changes their behavior after a transaction because they are insulated from risk.

Insurance Markets

Once insured, people may:

  • Take greater risks (e.g., leaving valuables in an unlocked car)
  • Engage in less preventive care (e.g., unhealthy lifestyle choices with health insurance)
  • File false claims

This increases the insurer's costs, leading to higher premiums and market inefficiency.

Real-World Example: Banking and Financial Crisis

Banks deemed "too big to fail" may take excessive risks knowing the government will bail them out. This moral hazard was evident in the 2008 financial crisis. The expectation of a bailout distorted risk-taking incentives.

Principal-Agent Problem

Moral hazard is closely related to the principal-agent problem, where:

  • The principal (e.g., shareholders) hires an agent (e.g., CEO)
  • The agent has better information about their efforts
  • The agent's interests may not align with the principal's
  • The agent may not work as hard or may pursue personal goals

Examples include:

  • Employees shirking when the boss isn't watching
  • CEOs pursuing empire-building over profit maximization
  • Politicians pursuing reelection over public welfare

Solutions to Asymmetric Information

Market-Based Solutions

  • Signaling: The informed party takes costly actions to reveal information (e.g., education as a signal of ability, warranties as a signal of quality)
  • Screening: The uninformed party designs contracts to separate types (e.g., insurance deductibles to screen risk types)
  • Reputation and repeat business: Firms build reputations to overcome information problems
  • Intermediaries: Third parties certify quality (e.g., car inspections, credit rating agencies)

Government Interventions

  • Mandatory disclosure: Requiring sellers to reveal information (e.g., food labeling, financial statements)
  • Licensing and certification: Ensuring minimum quality standards (e.g., medical licenses)
  • Consumer protection laws: Penalizing fraud and misrepresentation
  • Regulation: Direct oversight (e.g., banking regulation, safety standards)
  • Compulsory insurance: Preventing adverse selection (e.g., mandatory car insurance)

Welfare Effects

Asymmetric information causes market failure because:

  • Mutually beneficial trades do not occur (allocative inefficiency)
  • Resources are misallocated (wrong mix of goods produced)
  • Social welfare is below the optimum

Interventions can improve welfare but may also create costs (compliance, reduced innovation, moral hazard from regulation itself).

HL Exam Applications

You should be able to:

  • Distinguish between adverse selection and moral hazard
  • Explain how asymmetric information causes market failure
  • Apply these concepts to real-world markets (insurance, finance, labor, used goods)
  • Evaluate market-based and government solutions
  • Discuss the principal-agent problem in different contexts

Summary

Asymmetric information is a major source of market failure:

  • Adverse selection occurs when hidden information before a transaction distorts market outcomes (e.g., lemons problem, insurance)
  • Moral hazard occurs when hidden actions after a transaction change behavior (e.g., insurance, banking)
  • Both problems reduce efficiency and can lead to market collapse
  • Solutions include signaling, screening, regulation, and mandatory disclosure
  • Understanding information asymmetries is crucial for analyzing real-world markets
