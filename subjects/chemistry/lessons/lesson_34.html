Lesson 34: Electrochemical Cells — Voltaic Cells, E⦵ & Spontaneity

Lesson 34: Electrochemical Cells — Voltaic Cells, E⦵ & Spontaneity

Syllabus reference: 9.2, 19.1 (HL) — Voltaic Cells, Standard Electrode Potentials, ΔG⦵ and E⦵

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the construction and operation of a voltaic (galvanic) cell, identifying anode, cathode, and direction of electron flow.
  • Use standard electrode potentials ($E^{\ominus}$) to calculate cell potential ($E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$).
  • Predict whether a redox reaction is spontaneous using $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ values.
  • Relate $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ to the Gibbs free energy change: $\Delta G^{\ominus} = -nFE^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$.
  • Write cell diagrams using standard notation: $\ce{Zn(s) | Zn^{2+}(aq) || Cu^{2+}(aq) | Cu(s)}$.

1. Prior Knowledge Check

Question 1: In a redox reaction, where do electrons flow — from the reducing agent to the oxidising agent, or vice versa?

▸ Show
Electrons flow from the reducing agent (which loses electrons / is oxidised) to the oxidising agent (which gains electrons / is reduced).

Question 2: What does a positive $\Delta G$ tell you about the spontaneity of a reaction?

▸ Show
A positive $\Delta G$ means the reaction is non-spontaneous under the given conditions. A negative $\Delta G$ means spontaneous. $\Delta G = 0$ means the system is at equilibrium.

2. The Voltaic (Galvanic) Cell

A voltaic cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy by physically separating the oxidation and reduction half-reactions. Electrons are forced to travel through an external wire, producing an electric current.

Figure 1: The Daniell cell. Zn is oxidised at the anode (−); Cu²⁺ is reduced at the cathode (+). The salt bridge completes the circuit by allowing ion migration.

2.1 Key Components

ComponentRole
AnodeElectrode where oxidation occurs. In a voltaic cell, it is the negative electrode (electrons are produced here).
CathodeElectrode where reduction occurs. In a voltaic cell, it is the positive electrode (electrons are consumed here).
Salt bridgeA tube containing an inert electrolyte (e.g., $\ce{KNO3}$) that allows ions to migrate, maintaining electrical neutrality. Without it, charge builds up and the cell stops.
External wireConducts electrons from anode to cathode. This is where we extract useful electrical work.

Check-in: In the Daniell cell, why does the zinc electrode lose mass while the copper electrode gains mass?

▸ Show
At the anode: $\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^{2+}(aq) + 2e-}$. Solid zinc dissolves into solution as Zn²⁺ ions — the electrode loses mass. At the cathode: $\ce{Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}$. Cu²⁺ ions from solution are deposited as solid copper on the electrode — it gains mass.

3. Standard Electrode Potentials ($E^{\ominus}$)

The standard electrode potential ($E^{\ominus}$) measures the tendency of a half-cell to undergo reduction under standard conditions (298 K, 1 mol dm⁻³, 100 kPa). All values are measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which is assigned $E^{\ominus} = 0.00\text{ V}$.

Key principles of $E^{\ominus}$:
• More positive $E^{\ominus}$ = stronger oxidising agent (greater tendency to be reduced).
• More negative $E^{\ominus}$ = stronger reducing agent (greater tendency to be oxidised).
• $E^{\ominus}$ is an intensive property — it does NOT depend on the amount of substance.
• $E^{\ominus}$ values are NOT multiplied when you multiply a half-equation.
Figure 2: The electrochemical series. Species with more negative E⦵ are stronger reducing agents (easily oxidised). Species with more positive E⦵ are stronger oxidising agents (easily reduced).

3.1 Calculating Cell Potential

$$E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\ominus}_{\text{cathode}} - E^{\ominus}_{\text{anode}}$$

Or equivalently: $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\ominus}_{\text{reduction}} + E^{\ominus}_{\text{oxidation}}$, where $E^{\ominus}_{\text{oxidation}} = -E^{\ominus}_{\text{reduction}}$.

For a spontaneous reaction: $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} > 0$. If $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} < 0$, the reaction is non-spontaneous in that direction.

Check-in: Calculate $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ for $\ce{Fe^{2+} + Zn -> Fe + Zn^{2+}}$. ($E^{\ominus}$: $\ce{Fe^{2+}/Fe}$ = −0.45 V, $\ce{Zn^{2+}/Zn}$ = −0.76 V). Is it spontaneous?

▸ Show
Fe²⁺ is reduced (cathode, $E^{\ominus}$ = −0.45 V). Zn is oxidised (anode, $E^{\ominus}$ = −0.76 V). $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = −0.45 − (−0.76) = \mathbf{+0.31\text{ V}}$. Since $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} > 0$, the reaction is spontaneous.

4. Relationship Between $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ and $\Delta G^{\ominus}$

The cell potential is directly related to the maximum electrical work the cell can perform:

Key formula: $$\Delta G^{\ominus} = -nFE^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$$ $n$ = number of moles of electrons transferred, $F$ = Faraday constant = $9.65 \times 10^4\text{ C mol}^{-1}$, $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ in volts.

Implications: $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} > 0 \Rightarrow \Delta G^{\ominus} < 0$ (spontaneous). $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} < 0 \Rightarrow \Delta G^{\ominus} > 0$ (non-spontaneous).

Check-in: For the Daniell cell (Zn/Cu²⁺), $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = 1.10\text{ V}$ and $n = 2$. Calculate $\Delta G^{\ominus}$.

▸ Show
$\Delta G^{\ominus} = -nFE^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = -(2)(96500)(1.10) = \mathbf{-212\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$. The negative value confirms the reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous.

5. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Predicting Spontaneity:

Will $\ce{Cu}$ displace $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ from solution? $E^{\ominus}$: $\ce{Cu^{2+}/Cu}$ = +0.34 V, $\ce{Zn^{2+}/Zn}$ = −0.76 V.

Proposed reaction: $\ce{Cu + Zn^{2+} -> Cu^{2+} + Zn}$. Cu is oxidised (anode, $E^{\ominus}$ = +0.34 V). Zn²⁺ is reduced (cathode, $E^{\ominus}$ = −0.76 V).
$E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\ominus}_{\text{cathode}} - E^{\ominus}_{\text{anode}} = -0.76 - 0.34 = \mathbf{-1.10\text{ V}}$. Not spontaneous. This is the reverse of the Daniell cell. Copper cannot displace zinc from solution.

⚠️ Common wrong approach: Adding the potentials instead of subtracting: −0.76 + 0.34 = −0.42 V — still negative but numerically wrong. Always use cathode − anode.
Example 2 — Identifying Oxidising/Reducing Strength:

Given: $\ce{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}$ = +0.77 V, $\ce{I2/I-}$ = +0.54 V, $\ce{Br2/Br-}$ = +1.07 V. (a) Which is the strongest oxidising agent? (b) Will $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$ oxidise $\ce{I-}$?

(a) Strongest oxidising agent = most positive $E^{\ominus}$ = $\ce{Br2}$ (+1.07 V).
(b) $\ce{2Fe^{3+} + 2I- -> 2Fe^{2+} + I2}$. Cathode: $\ce{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}$ (+0.77 V). Anode: $\ce{I2/I-}$ (+0.54 V). $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = 0.77 - 0.54 = +0.23\text{ V} > 0$ → Yes, spontaneous.

⚠️ Common wrong approach: Comparing $E^{\ominus}$ values without considering which is cathode and which is anode. For a spontaneous reaction, the species with the higher (more positive) $E^{\ominus}$ must be reduced.
Example 3 — Cell Diagram Notation:

Write the cell diagram for a cell where $\ce{Mg}$ is oxidised to $\ce{Mg^{2+}}$ and $\ce{Ag+}$ is reduced to $\ce{Ag}$.

Convention: $\ce{anode | anode\ solution || cathode\ solution | cathode}$
Anode (oxidation): $\ce{Mg(s) | Mg^{2+}(aq)}$
Cathode (reduction): $\ce{Ag+(aq) | Ag(s)}$
Cell diagram: $\ce{Mg(s) | Mg^{2+}(aq) || Ag+(aq) | Ag(s)}$.
$E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\ominus}(\ce{Ag+/Ag}) - E^{\ominus}(\ce{Mg^{2+}/Mg}) = +0.80 - (-2.37) = +3.17\text{ V}$.

⚠️ Common wrong approach: Reversing the order — putting the cathode on the left. The convention is always anode (oxidation) on the left, cathode (reduction) on the right.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Multiplying $E^{\ominus}$ by the stoichiometric coefficient. $E^{\ominus}$ is an intensive property. Even if you multiply a half-equation by 2, the $E^{\ominus}$ stays the same. Do NOT multiply potentials.
  2. Confusing anode/cathode signs. In a voltaic cell: anode = (−), cathode = (+). In an electrolytic cell, they are reversed. Know which type you're dealing with.
  3. Using $E^{\ominus}$ values under non-standard conditions. $E^{\ominus}$ applies at 298 K, 1 mol dm⁻³, 100 kPa. Under non-standard conditions, use the Nernst equation (HL only): $E = E^{\ominus} - (RT/nF)\ln Q$.
  4. Forgetting the salt bridge. A cell without a salt bridge will not work — charge builds up immediately and the reaction stops. The salt bridge completes the circuit.

6. End-of-Lesson Practice

Question 1 (Paper 1 style): In a voltaic cell, electrons flow from:
A. Cathode to anode   B. Anode to cathode   C. Positive to negative   D. Salt bridge to electrodes

Question 2: Calculate $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ and predict spontaneity for $\ce{2Al + 3Cu^{2+} -> 2Al^{3+} + 3Cu}$. ($E^{\ominus}$: $\ce{Al^{3+}/Al}$ = −1.66 V, $\ce{Cu^{2+}/Cu}$ = +0.34 V)

Question 3: Will $\ce{Ag}$ dissolve in 1 mol dm⁻³ $\ce{HCl}$? ($E^{\ominus}$: $\ce{Ag+/Ag}$ = +0.80 V, $\ce{2H+/H2}$ = 0.00 V)

Question 4: Write the cell diagram and calculate $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$ for: $\ce{Ni^{2+} + Fe -> Ni + Fe^{2+}}$. ($E^{\ominus}$: $\ce{Ni^{2+}/Ni}$ = −0.26 V, $\ce{Fe^{2+}/Fe}$ = −0.45 V)

Question 5: Calculate $\Delta G^{\ominus}$ for the reaction in Q4. ($F = 96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}$)

Question 6 (IB-style): Using the data $\ce{MnO4-/Mn^{2+}}$ = +1.51 V and $\ce{Cl2/Cl-}$ = +1.36 V, explain why acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ can oxidise $\ce{Cl-}$ to $\ce{Cl2}$.

Answer 1: B. Electrons flow from anode (where they're produced by oxidation) to cathode (where they're consumed by reduction). This is from negative to positive in the external circuit.

Answer 2: Al is oxidised (anode, −1.66 V). Cu²⁺ is reduced (cathode, +0.34 V). $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = +0.34 - (-1.66) = \mathbf{+2.00\text{ V}}$. $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} > 0$ → spontaneous. Note: do NOT multiply by coefficients.

Answer 3: $\ce{2Ag + 2H+ -> 2Ag+ + H2}$. Ag oxidised (anode, +0.80 V). H⁺ reduced (cathode, 0.00 V). $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = 0.00 - 0.80 = -0.80\text{ V}$. Negative → not spontaneous. Silver does NOT dissolve in HCl. (It does dissolve in HNO₃ because NO₃⁻ is a stronger oxidising agent.)

Answer 4: Fe oxidised (anode), Ni²⁺ reduced (cathode). Diagram: $\ce{Fe(s) | Fe^{2+}(aq) || Ni^{2+}(aq) | Ni(s)}$. $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = -0.26 - (-0.45) = \mathbf{+0.19\text{ V}}$.

Answer 5: $n = 2$ (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻). $\Delta G^{\ominus} = -(2)(96500)(0.19) = \mathbf{-36.7\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$.

Answer 6: $\ce{2MnO4- + 10Cl- + 16H+ -> 2Mn^{2+} + 5Cl2 + 8H2O}$. Cathode: MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺ (+1.51 V). Anode: Cl₂/Cl⁻ (+1.36 V). $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} = 1.51 - 1.36 = +0.15\text{ V} > 0$, so the reaction is spontaneous. The more positive potential (MnO₄⁻) oxidises the less positive one (Cl⁻).

7. Key Terms

TermDefinition

AnodeThe electrode where oxidation occurs; negative in a voltaic cell
CathodeThe electrode where reduction occurs; positive in a voltaic cell
$E^{\ominus}$Standard electrode potential — the reduction potential measured under standard conditions vs. SHE
SHEStandard Hydrogen Electrode: $\ce{2H+(aq, 1M) + 2e- <=> H2(g, 100 kPa)}$, assigned $E^{\ominus} = 0.00\text{ V}$
$E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$$E^{\ominus}_{\text{cathode}} - E^{\ominus}_{\text{anode}}$; positive for spontaneous reactions
Salt bridgeAn ion-conducting connection (e.g., filter paper soaked in KNO₃) that completes the circuit

8. Exam Tips

Paper 1 (Multiple Choice): Expect questions testing whether you know $E^{\ominus}$ is NOT multiplied by coefficients. A common distractor gives a cell potential that has been incorrectly multiplied. Also, questions on electron flow direction: always anode → cathode in the external wire.
Paper 2 (Structured): When asked to predict spontaneity, always: (1) identify which species is oxidised and which is reduced, (2) write the appropriate $E^{\ominus}$ values, (3) calculate $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}}$, (4) state that $E^{\ominus}_{\text{cell}} > 0$ means spontaneous. Showing these four steps earns full marks.
Data Booklet: The IB Data Booklet contains a table of standard electrode potentials (Section 24). All values are written as reduction half-equations (oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form). Be careful when you need the oxidation potential — you must reverse the sign.
