Lesson 3: Electron Configuration — Orbitals, Aufbau, Hund, Pauli

Lesson 3: Electron Configuration — Orbitals, Aufbau, Hund, Pauli

Syllabus reference: 2.3 (Electron Configurations)

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the shape and capacity of s, p, and d orbitals.
  • Write full and condensed electron configurations for atoms and ions up to Z = 36.
  • Apply the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule, and the Pauli exclusion principle.
  • Explain the anomalous configurations of chromium and copper.

1. Prior Knowledge Check

Q1: How many electrons can the second shell (n = 2) hold?

▸ Show
8 electrons: 2 in the 2s subshell + 6 in the 2p subshell. The formula $2n^2$ gives $2(4) = 8$.

Q2: What does the atomic number (Z) tell you about a neutral atom's electrons?

▸ Show
In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals Z (the atomic number). For ions, adjust: add electrons for negative charge, subtract for positive charge.

2. Energy Levels, Subshells, and Orbitals

Electrons do not orbit randomly. They occupy specific energy levels (n = 1, 2, 3, 4…), and within each level are subshells (s, p, d, f). Each subshell contains orbitals — regions of space where electrons are most likely to be found.

Figure 1: The hierarchy of electron arrangement. Each energy level n contains n subshells. Each subshell contains specific numbers of orbitals, and each orbital holds up to 2 electrons.

2.1 Orbital Shapes

An s orbital is spherical. Every energy level has one s orbital. A p orbital is shaped like a dumbbell (two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus). Every level from n = 2 upward has three p orbitals, oriented along the x, y, and z axes. d orbitals (from n = 3 upward) have more complex cloverleaf shapes — there are five of them.

Figure 2: Approximate shapes. s = spherical, p = two-lobed dumbbell, d = four-lobed cloverleaf (one of five orientations shown).

Check-in: How many electrons can the 3d subshell hold? How many orbitals does it contain?

▸ Show
The d subshell has 5 orbitals, each holding 2 electrons → 10 electrons maximum.

3. The Three Rules for Filling Orbitals

3.1 The Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill orbitals starting from the lowest energy and moving upward. The order is NOT simply 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 3d. Because of subtle energy differences, 4s fills before 3d. The full order (up to Z = 36) is:

Figure 3: The Aufbau order. Yellow = 4s fills before 3d. Blue = 3d fills after 4s. This is the order for neutral atoms.

3.2 Hund's Rule

When filling orbitals of equal energy (like the three 2p orbitals), electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing up. Think of it like passengers on a bus: everyone takes an empty seat before sharing.

3.3 The Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers. Practically: each orbital can hold at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins (represented by ↑ and ↓ in orbital diagrams).

Figure 4: Nitrogen's 2p³ configuration. Each of the three p orbitals gets one electron with the same spin before any pairing occurs.

4. Writing Electron Configurations

4.1 Full Configuration

List every occupied subshell with the number of electrons as a superscript. Oxygen (Z = 8): $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^4}$.

4.2 Condensed (Noble Gas Core) Configuration

Replace the inner-shell configuration with the symbol of the preceding noble gas in brackets. Iron (Z = 26): $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^6}$.

4.3 The Cr and Cu Exceptions

Chromium (Z = 24) is not $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^4}$ — it is $\ce{[Ar] 4s^1 3d^5}$. Copper (Z = 29) is not $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^9}$ — it is $\ce{[Ar] 4s^1 3d^{10}}$. In both cases, one electron from 4s moves into 3d to create a half-filled (d⁵) or fully-filled (d¹⁰) d subshell. These configurations are more stable because half-filled and fully-filled d subshells have extra stability from exchange energy and symmetrical electron distribution.

Check-in: Write the full electron configuration of (a) Fe³⁺ and (b) Cu⁺. (Iron: Z = 26, Copper: Z = 29)

▸ Show
(a) Fe: $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^6}$. Remove 3 electrons: first from 4s (2 electrons), then from 3d (1 electron). Fe³⁺: $\ce{[Ar] 3d^5}$ or $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^5}$. (b) Cu: $\ce{[Ar] 4s^1 3d^{10}}$. Remove 1 electron from 4s: Cu⁺: $\ce{[Ar] 3d^{10}}$. Important: when forming cations, electrons are always removed from the highest n value first — 4s before 3d.

5. Worked Examples

Example 1: Write the full and condensed electron configurations of manganese (Z = 25).

Step 1: Count electrons. Neutral Mn has 25 electrons.
Step 2: Follow the Aufbau order. 1s² (2), 2s² (4), 2p⁶ (10), 3s² (12), 3p⁶ (18), 4s² (20), 3d⁵ (25).
Full: $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^5}$
Condensed: $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^5}$ (Ar has 18 electrons)

⚠️ Common wrong approach: Writing 3d before 4s in the full configuration. For neutral atoms, 4s fills before 3d, so the correct order is ...3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁵.
Example 2: Draw the orbital diagram for oxygen (Z = 8) and identify the two unpaired electrons.

Step 1: O = 1s² 2s² 2p⁴.
Step 2: Orbital diagram: 1s: [↑↓]   2s: [↑↓]   2p: [↑↓][↑][↑]
Step 3: The 2p subshell has 4 electrons. By Hund's rule: first electron in each orbital (3 electrons, all ↑), then the fourth pairs in the first orbital. Result: 2 unpaired electrons in the 2p subshell.
Example 3 (exam trap): A student writes the electron configuration of $\ce{V^{2+}}$ (vanadium, Z = 23) as $\ce{[Ar] 3d^3}$. Is this correct?

Step 1: Neutral V: $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^3}$ (23 electrons).
Step 2: To form $\ce{V^{2+}}$, remove 2 electrons. Electrons are lost from the highest energy level (n = 4) first, which is 4s.
Step 3: Remove both 4s electrons → $\ce{[Ar] 3d^3}$. Yes, the student is correct.

⚠️ Common wrong approach: Some students remove from 3d first because "3d is higher energy than 4s" — but for cations, the 4s electrons are lost first because they are in the outermost (highest n) shell.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Filling 3d before 4s for neutral atoms. For neutral atoms, 4s fills before 3d. Cr is $\ce{[Ar] 4s^1 3d^5}$, not $\ce{[Ar] 3d^5 4s^1}$ (though the order is less important in condensed form, examiners expect 4s written first for neutral atoms).
  2. Forgetting that 4s electrons are lost first when forming cations. Even though 4s fills before 3d, it is also the first to empty because it has the higher principal quantum number (n = 4 vs n = 3). Fe²⁺: $\ce{[Ar] 3d^6}$, NOT $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^4}$.
  3. Pairing electrons before filling all orbitals in a subshell. Hund's rule: for p³, all three p orbitals get one electron each (↑ ↑ ↑) before any pairing. For d⁵ in Cr, all five d orbitals get one electron.
  4. Memorizing Cr and Cu without understanding WHY. The driving force is the extra stability of half-filled (d⁵) and fully-filled (d¹⁰) d subshells. The energy gained by this arrangement slightly exceeds the energy cost of promoting a 4s electron.

6. End-of-Lesson Practice

Q1 (Paper 1): Which of the following electron configurations violates Hund's rule?
A. $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6}$   B. $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^2 2p_y^1}$   C. $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1}$   D. $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^4}$

Q2: Write the full electron configuration of (a) sulfur (Z = 16) and (b) $\ce{S^{2-}}$.

Q3: Draw the orbital diagram for carbon (Z = 6). How many unpaired electrons does it have?

Q4: Write condensed configurations for (a) $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ and (b) $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$. Explain why $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$ is more stable.

Q5: Explain why chromium's ground-state configuration is $\ce{[Ar] 4s^1 3d^5}$ rather than $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^4}$.

Q6 (IB-style): The $\ce{Ni^{2+}}$ ion has the configuration $\ce{[Ar] 3d^8}$. (a) Write the full electron configuration of neutral Ni (Z = 28) and explain which electrons were removed to form $\ce{Ni^{2+}}$. (b) The $\ce{V^{2+}}$ ion (vanadium, Z = 23) has the configuration $\ce{[Ar] 3d^3}$. Write the configuration of neutral V and explain whether the electrons removed came from 4s or 3d, and why they are removed in that order.

A1: B. Hund's rule states that electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing. In option B, the 2p subshell has 3 electrons but two are paired in the same orbital (2p_x² 2p_y¹) while 2p_z is empty. This violates Hund's rule — each of the three 2p orbitals should receive one electron before any pairing occurs.

A2: (a) S (Z=16): $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^4}$ or $\ce{[Ne] 3s^2 3p^4}$. (b) $\ce{S^{2-}}$ has 18 electrons: $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6}$ or $\ce{[Ar]}$.

A3: C: 1s² 2s² 2p². Orbitals: 1s[↑↓] 2s[↑↓] 2p[↑][↑][ ]. Two unpaired electrons in 2p.

A4: (a) $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$: $\ce{[Ar] 3d^6}$. (b) $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$: $\ce{[Ar] 3d^5}$. Fe³⁺ is more stable because it has a half-filled 3d subshell (d⁵), which gives extra stability — the same reason Cr adopts 3d⁵.

A5: A half-filled d subshell (d⁵) is unusually stable due to exchange energy and symmetrical electron distribution. By promoting one 4s electron to 3d, chromium achieves this stable half-filled configuration. The energy gained outweighs the small cost of the electron promotion.

A6: (a) Neutral Ni (Z=28): $\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^8}$ or $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^8}$. To form $\ce{Ni^{2+}}$, the two 4s electrons are removed because 4s has the higher principal quantum number (n=4 vs n=3). Electrons are always removed from the outermost shell (highest n) first, regardless of the order in which the orbitals were filled. (b) Neutral V (Z=23): $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^3}$. To form $\ce{V^{2+}}$, the two 4s electrons are removed for the same reason — they are in the outermost shell (n=4). This leaves $\ce{[Ar] 3d^3}$. The 3d electrons (n=3) are closer to the nucleus and are only removed after all 4s electrons are gone. This is a common exam point: for transition metal cations, 4s electrons are ALWAYS lost before 3d electrons, even though 4s fills before 3d in neutral atoms.

7. Key Terms

TermDefinition
Energy level (n)A principal shell occupied by electrons (n = 1, 2, 3...)
SubshellA subdivision of an energy level: s, p, d, f
OrbitalA region of space where up to 2 electrons are likely to be found
Aufbau principleElectrons fill orbitals from lowest energy to highest
Hund's ruleElectrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing
Pauli exclusion principleNo two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers; each orbital holds max 2 e⁻ with opposite spins
Condensed configurationElectron configuration using a noble gas core, e.g., $\ce{[Ar] 4s^2 3d^6}$

8. Exam Tips

Paper 1: Questions testing Hund's rule and the Pauli exclusion principle often show orbital diagrams or configurations with a subtle violation. Check: (1) are all orbitals in a subshell singly occupied before pairing? (2) does any orbital have more than 2 electrons? (3) are paired electrons shown with opposite spins?
Paper 2: When writing configurations for ions, the exam expects you to remove electrons from the highest n first (4s before 3d). This is worth a mark. State this explicitly: "Electrons are removed from the 4s orbital because it has the highest principal quantum number."
